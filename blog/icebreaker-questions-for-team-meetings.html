25 icebreaker questions for team meetings (that people actually enjoy)

Grouped by when to actually use them — not one more undifferentiated list of "what's your favorite color" filler.

8-minute read · for meeting hosts, managers and remote teams

Most icebreaker lists are the same forty questions copy-pasted across a hundred blog posts, sorted by nothing in particular. The problem isn't that icebreakers are cheesy — it's that the wrong one for the moment feels forced, and a forced icebreaker is worse than skipping it entirely. A "what's your spirit animal" question lands fine at a Friday social and dies on arrival in a Monday 9am status meeting with three people who've never met.

Below are 25 questions actually grouped by situation: light warm-ups, real get-to-know-you questions for newer teams, ones built specifically for remote calls, ones that double as useful management information, and would-you-rather questions for a quick jolt of energy mid-meeting. Pick from the category that matches the room, not the whole list at once.

Light & fun

Use these to open a meeting where the goal is just to get people talking before anything serious happens — a weekly standup, a kickoff, or any meeting where the room is quiet and a little cold.

  1. What's the most useless talent you have?
  2. If your job title were based on your last message in the team chat instead of your actual role, what would it be?
  3. What's a food combination you love that everyone else thinks is disgusting?
  4. What's the last thing you looked up online that you'd be mildly embarrassed to admit?
  5. If you had to survive a zombie apocalypse with only the people in this meeting, what job would you assign yourself?

Get-to-know-you

Use these when the team has new members, has been fully remote long enough that people only know each other's job titles, or you're running proper onboarding and want people to leave the meeting actually knowing something about each other.

  1. What's a hobby you have that would genuinely surprise people on this team?
  2. What's the best piece of advice you've ever been given — that you ignored?
  3. What was your very first job, and what's one thing it taught you that still applies today?
  4. Who's someone outside of work who shaped how you approach your job?
  5. What's a small, ordinary thing that reliably puts you in a good mood?

Remote-team-specific

Use these on video calls specifically — they're designed to surface the small context a shared office gives people for free, like what's on someone's desk or what their afternoon actually looks like, which a purely verbal call otherwise strips out.

  1. What's something visible in your workspace right now that says something about you?
  2. What time zone do you secretly wish everyone else would just switch to?
  3. What's your go-to trick for staying alert through the 3pm slump?
  4. Coffee, tea, energy drink, or something weirder — what's actually in your mug right now?
  5. What's one thing about working from home that nobody warned you about before you started?

Work-preference

Use these when a team is still forming how it works together, or a new manager wants to actually learn how people prefer to collaborate rather than just meet them — the answers are genuinely useful, not just social.

  1. Do you do your best thinking during a meeting, or afterward, alone?
  2. Morning person or night owl — and does your calendar actually reflect that?
  3. Do you prefer getting feedback in writing, or said out loud?
  4. What's your honest ideal number of meetings per week?
  5. Would you rather have one extra hour of uninterrupted focus time, or one fewer meeting?

Would-you-rather style

Use these for a quick mid-meeting energy reset, or as a structured two-minute activity — forcing a binary choice tends to generate more actual back-and-forth than an open-ended question does, because people want to defend their pick.

  1. Would you rather sing every request you make for a day, or only communicate in emoji for a week?
  2. Would you rather always show up 10 minutes early, or always finish 10 minutes early?
  3. Would you rather have unlimited PTO you're always too busy to use, or two extra guaranteed weeks off?
  4. Would you rather give up your phone's camera or its calendar, permanently?
  5. Would you rather work with someone brilliant but disorganized, or reliable but average?

How to actually run these without it being awkward

The format matters as much as the question. Going around the room one by one, in order, turns a two-minute warm-up into a ten-minute obligation where the last three people are only half-listening because they're rehearsing their own answer. A few things that consistently work better:

  • Cap it at one question, most weeks. A single sharp question people actually enjoy answering beats three mediocre ones that start to feel like homework.
  • Make it optional, out loud. "Pass" should always be a fine answer — some people genuinely don't want to share on a given day, and forcing it teaches people to dread the start of your meetings.
  • Rotate who picks the question. If it's always the manager choosing, it starts to feel like an exercise being run on the team rather than something the team is doing together.
  • Let people answer at the same time instead of one at a time. Simultaneous answers remove the "rehearsing while waiting" problem and are simply faster for a large group.

A good rule of thumb across all five categories: if you wouldn't be comfortable answering the question yourself in front of the team, don't ask it of anyone else. Icebreakers work when they lower the temperature in the room, not when they turn into a mild interrogation.

Frequently asked questions

How many icebreaker questions should we do per meeting?

One is usually right. A single well-chosen question keeps the warm-up to a couple of minutes; stacking two or three starts to eat into the actual meeting and people begin dreading the start of it.

Are icebreaker questions actually worth the time?

Only when they're specific and optional. A generic question people have answered a dozen times elsewhere feels like wasted time; a well-picked one from a category that matches the room genuinely warms people up faster than jumping straight into the agenda cold.

How do I run these on a video call without it feeling awkward?

Let people answer at the same time rather than going around one by one — going in order means everyone at the end of the queue is only half-listening while they wait their turn. A tool like PollSlide that collects everyone's answer on their own phone at once removes that wait entirely.

What if someone doesn't want to answer?

Let them pass without comment, every time. The moment "pass" stops being a real option, icebreakers stop being optional fun and start being a mild obligation — which defeats the entire point of asking.
